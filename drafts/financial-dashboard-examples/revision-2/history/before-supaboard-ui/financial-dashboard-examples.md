Sales are up. Margin is up. So why is the finance team still chasing payments?

A dashboard can report a successful month while leaving a serious collections problem out of view. Revenue and gross margin describe what the business earned. Neither tells you whether customers paid on time.

**A financial dashboard should show what changed in the business, how it compares with the previous period or plan, and which records explain the difference. Revenue, margin, receivables and cash answer different questions.**

The three examples below follow one fictional business through a profitability review, an overdue receivables investigation and a combined finance review. All figures are illustrative USD amounts.

## 1. Profitability dashboard: is sales growth more profitable?

September looks better than August. Revenue rose from $500,000 to $600,000, and gross margin improved from 35% to 38%. For a founder reviewing commercial performance, both are welcome results.

<!-- FIGURE: performance -->

This dashboard earns its place in the review when the next question is **“Which products or customers improved revenue and margin?”** Breakdowns by product and customer help distinguish higher sales volume from price changes, discounting or a shift toward more profitable work. Revenue entries and cost-of-sales records provide the evidence for pricing and sales decisions.

But a stronger gross margin does not establish that money arrived in the bank. Under accrual accounting, revenue can be recognized before a customer pays. The [SEC’s guide to financial statements](https://www.sec.gov/about/reports-publications/beginners-guide-financial-statements) makes the distinction clear: profitability and cash generation are different questions.

## 2. Accounts receivable dashboard: which payments need attention?

At September’s close, overdue receivables stand at $160,000, twice August’s $80,000. An **accounts receivable dashboard** should show the overdue total, its age, the customers responsible and the invoices requiring action.

The aging chart reveals something the total alone cannot: balances more than 30 days overdue have tripled, from $30,000 to $90,000. That is a stronger reason to investigate than the headline increase alone.

<!-- FIGURE: aging -->

An **accounts receivable aging report** groups unpaid balances into age bands. Here, age means days past the contractual due date; invoices not yet due are excluded from the overdue chart. [ACCA’s receivables guidance](https://www.accaglobal.com/sg/en/student/exam-support-resources/fundamentals-exams-study-resources/f9/technical-articles/arm.html) explains how aged analysis identifies customer balances that have breached credit terms.

The review question becomes **“Which customers account for the older balances, and what is stopping payment?”** In this example, Customer A accounts for $45,000 of September’s $55,000 in the 31–60-day band. The latest collection notes point to three different follow-ups:

<!-- FIGURE: invoices -->

Identical reminders would leave the reported reference problem and delivery dispute unresolved. Each needs a different owner.

Each record needs an invoice ID, due date, remaining balance, dated collection note and next-action owner. Receipts and credits should be linked so the team can verify how the balance changed. A promise to pay is still a note until payment is received.

This view identifies a collections problem. It does not forecast cash or establish the size of a cash shortfall; payroll, inventory purchases, supplier payments and debt repayments also affect the bank balance.

## 3. Combined dashboard: what needs a decision?

A combined finance dashboard puts profitability and collections alongside one another, so improving sales cannot hide overdue payments. Its value comes from preserving the question behind each measure. Revenue growth may call for a sales decision; an old unpaid invoice may need an operational fix.

<!-- FIGURE: review -->

Use this worksheet to choose [financial KPIs](https://supaboard.ai/blog/what-is-a-kpi) and the detail that should sit behind them:

| Measure | Review question → decision | Comparison | Supporting detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | Where did growth come from? → Adjust sales priorities. | Prior comparable period | Customer and product revenue; recognition entries |
| Gross margin | What changed profitability? → Review pricing or costs. | Prior period, same cost definition | Discounts, product mix and cost-of-sales entries |
| Overdue receivables | What is blocking payment? → Assign collection or dispute work. | Prior snapshot, by age band | Invoices, receipts, credits and collection notes |
| Cash balance | What drove the change? → Review spending and payment timing. | Opening balance plus actual inflows less outflows | Bank transactions and reconciliation |
| Expense variance | Why did spending differ? → Correct, defer or approve costs. | Actual against budget | Ledger entries, suppliers and accruals |

To explain a change in cash, trace money that actually moved through the bank. Receivables alone cannot reconcile that change. Likewise, a fall in overdue balances may reflect credit notes or write-offs rather than cash collected.

Keep comparisons consistent: revenue covers a period; receivables describe a position at a particular date. A September receivables view must include older unpaid invoices, not just invoices issued in September. Put the entity, currency, reporting date and last refresh beside the measures.

For the review agenda, check that scope first, discuss the material changes, open the records behind them, then agree an owner and next review date. Customer A’s delivery dispute now has a named action instead of disappearing inside a red total.
