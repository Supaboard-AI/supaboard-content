Financial dashboard examples: green KPIs, tight cash

Dashboard exampleReceivablesKPI worksheetSupaboard

Revenue is growing, gross margin is improving, and every KPI on the dashboard is green. Yet the business is chasing customers for payments. The figures can all be correct: the dashboard measures profitability but leaves payment timing out of the picture.

Useful financial dashboard examples combine revenue, profitability, receivables and actual cash movements. Each measure should answer a review question and connect to the records needed to investigate it. For finance managers, founders and operations leaders, that connection makes the difference between reporting good results and understanding a cash problem.

Why can sales and margins rise while cash gets tight?

Revenue records what a business has earned; cash receipts record what customers have paid. Under accrual accounting, a credit sale can increase revenue before payment arrives. Gross margin measures the share of revenue remaining after cost of sales, so it can improve while invoices remain unpaid.

The SEC’s guide to financial statements distinguishes these questions: an income statement shows profitability, while a cash flow statement shows whether the business generated cash. A profitable month does not necessarily produce a stronger bank balance.

Overdue receivables are therefore a useful place to investigate when growth coincides with collection pressure. They identify money customers already owe beyond agreed payment terms. They do not, by themselves, explain the entire cash position: inventory, payroll, supplier payments and debt repayments matter too.

A financial dashboard example with a missing collections view

Consider a business whose monthly dashboard initially shows only revenue and gross margin. Adding overdue receivables reveals a different pattern:

Measure August September What changed?
Recognized revenue $500,000 $600,000 Up 20%
Gross margin 35% 38% Up 3 percentage points
Overdue receivables at month-end $80,000 $160,000 Doubled

Illustrative USD figures for a fictional business, not customer results or benchmarks.

Revenue and margin improved, but twice as much money remained unpaid after its due date. The original dashboard had no measure for that deterioration. Its green cards described a narrower success than the team assumed.

The overdue increase also needs explaining. In this example, $110,000 became newly overdue during September, while receipts reduced overdue balances by $25,000 and credit notes reduced them by $5,000. That reconciles the movement: $80,000 + $110,000 − $25,000 − $5,000 = $160,000.

This comparison identifies a collections problem worth investigating. It is not a cash forecast, and the $80,000 increase is not a calculation of the business’s cash shortfall.

What should an accounts receivable dashboard show?

An accounts receivable dashboard should show outstanding balances, overdue amounts, aging bands and the customers behind them. An accounts receivable aging report groups unpaid invoices by age; for a collections review, define the bands using days past the contractual due date. Separate invoices that are not yet due from those that are overdue.

ACCA’s guidance on receivables management explains how aged analysis identifies outstanding customer balances and breaches of credit terms. The practical next step is to connect those balances to invoice and collection records.

In the fictional business, Customer A accounts for $45,000 of the newly overdue amount. Collection notes flag a missing purchase-order reference on a $20,000 invoice, a delivery dispute on a $15,000 invoice, and no payment confirmation on a $10,000 invoice.

Those notes suggest different actions. Billing must verify the purchase-order reference, operations must check delivery evidence, and collections must follow up on the remaining balance. A single instruction to “chase payments” would obscure those differences.

Supporting records should include invoice IDs, due dates, unpaid amounts, applied receipts, credits, note timestamps and an action owner. Missing due dates belong in an exception list. A customer’s promise to pay belongs in the notes, separate from a confirmed receipt.

Match financial dashboard KPIs to decisions

A profitability dashboard, an accounts receivable dashboard and a cash flow dashboard serve different purposes. The first explains earnings, the second supports collections, and the third tracks actual money movements. A finance review can combine these views without treating them as interchangeable.

Use this metric-to-decision worksheet when choosing financial KPIs:

Measure Review question Comparison Supporting records
Revenue What drove growth, and should sales priorities change? Prior comparable period Revenue entries, recognition dates, credits
Gross margin What changed margin, and should pricing or costs change? Prior period, same cost definition Product revenue, cost-of-sales entries, discounts
Overdue receivables Which missed payments need action? Prior snapshot and balance movements Invoices, receipts, credits, collection notes
Cash balance What explains the cash change? Opening balance plus actual inflows less outflows Bank transactions and reconciliation
Expense variance Which costs need investigation? Actual against budget Ledger entries, suppliers, accruals

The comparison needs a consistent basis. Monthly revenue covers a period; overdue receivables are measured at a snapshot date. Filtering September receivables to invoices issued in September would wrongly exclude older unpaid bills. Display the period, entity, currency and refresh time alongside the numbers.

A useful review agenda follows the same logic: check definitions and freshness, discuss material changes, inspect the supporting records, then assign actions and review dates. When overdue balances fall, distinguish collections from credits or write-offs; only collections bring in cash.
